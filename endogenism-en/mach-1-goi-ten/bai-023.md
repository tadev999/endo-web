# Why Do We Need Validation from Strangers More Than from Ourselves?

*Article 23 / Stream 1: Naming*

---

You just completed something — an essay, a project, something into which you poured considerable effort.

And the initial reaction isn't pausing to feel it. But rather: *should I post this? What will others think?*

You post. Then you wait. Likes arrive — and a subtle sense of relief occurs, almost physiological, each time that number increments. A positive comment appears — and a warmth spreads that you don't fully comprehend why it carries such strength.

And if nothing occurs — if likes are sparse, if no one comments — a sense of disappointment surfaces, though you know intellectually that the quality of what you created didn't alter simply because of like counts.

I used to check a post multiple times during the initial hour after posting — not needing any information, but simply to inspect whether numbers changed.

---

## The Brain Is Programmed to Need Validation

Before delving deeper into the matter, one thing must be understood: the desire for validation is no modern weakness. It is an ancient and deeply embedded component of the human brain.

Throughout most of human history, social validation from one's tribe was no luxury item — it was a matter of survival. Being accepted by the tribe meant protection, shared resources, safety. Being rejected by the tribe meant genuine danger — lacking communal protection in a high-risk world.

Thus, the human brain evolved to be exceptionally sensitive to signals of validation and rejection from others. That is no design flaw — it is a system that helped our ancestors survive.

The issue is that system was designed for a world vastly different from our present world — a world where your social group consisted of a few dozen people you knew intimately, not hundreds or thousands on a screen, the vast majority of whom you have never met and never will.

---

## When an Old System Meets a New Environment

Social media accomplished something exceptionally effective — and deeply problematic: it took the brain's ancient social validation system and placed it into a completely different environment, where validation arrives from strangers, is quantified by numbers, and is available in ways unprecedented in human history.

In the natural environment for which the brain was designed, validation arrived from a small group knowing you genuinely — and that validation reflected something meaningful: they knew you, had observed you over time, their acceptance rested on a genuine relationship.

Validation on social media is fundamentally different. A like from a stranger doesn't reflect that they comprehend you — it typically reflects only that a photo or phrase aligned with their aesthetic taste during a few seconds of scrolling. Yet the brain, still operating by ancient logic, fails to distinguish that difference. It receives the validation signal and responds as though it were genuine, meaningful validation from an important community.

That is why a like from a total stranger — someone knowing nothing about you beyond that photo — can still generate genuine pleasant sensations. Not because you are naive or shallow. But because the system within your brain is responding precisely according to its design, only operating within an environment it was never created to process.

---

## When External Validation Replaces Internal Validation

Something far more dangerous exists than simply enjoying validation: when external validation gradually and completely replaces the capacity for internal self-validation.

That occurs in subtle ways. Initially, you create something because you find it meaningful — then share it wanting others to see it as well. Yet over time, that sequence can invert: you begin creating something with the implicit question *"will this be validated"* placed ahead of *"is this meaningful to me."*

When that occurs long enough, you gradually lose the capacity to evaluate what holds worth without first inspecting others' reactions. You write something and aren't certain whether it is good until observing like counts. You perform a good deed and feel it only truly "counts" if someone knows and validates it. You reach an achievement and the sense of pride arrives genuinely only after sharing it and receiving positive feedback.

Here precisely lies the danger: not the desire to be validated — that is natural — but losing the capacity to validate oneself, to the extent that one's sense of worth regarding a deed rests entirely on external reactions.

---

## The Difference Between Sharing and Seeking Confirmation

A boundary exists — not always obvious, yet worth noting — between sharing something out of the joy of sharing, and needing confirmation to know whether something holds worth.

Sharing out of joy looks like this: you already felt satisfied or proud regarding something prior to sharing it. The act of sharing is an extension of that feeling — not its origin. Even if no one responds, the initial sense of worth regarding that deed remains intact.

Needing confirmation looks different: the sense of worth doesn't genuinely form until external feedback arrives. Prior to posting, anxiety exists — not knowing if this is "good enough." After posting, a tense waiting period ensues. And feedback, whether positive or absent, decides the final sense of worth regarding the deed — not the deed itself.

Noticing where you stand on this spectrum is not for judging yourself — most of us occupy somewhere between the two poles, and that is entirely human. But noticing it is the initial step toward rebuilding the capacity for self-validation — the capacity to know something holds meaning without needing any numbers to confirm it.

---

## Rebuilding the Capacity for Self-Validation

The capacity for self-validation didn't vanish naturally — it was simply crowded out by an easier and continuous source of external validation. And like any crowded-out capacity, it can be rebuilt — not by severing entirely from external validation, but by practicing paying attention to internal feelings prior to reaching outward.

That can begin very small: doing something and choosing not to share it immediately — not forever, simply permitting the sense of worth regarding it to form first, from within yourself, prior to inviting others in. Completing a task and taking a moment to ask *"how do I feel about this"* prior to asking *"what will others think."* Noticing when the impulse to share stems from genuine joy versus when it stems from anxiety needing soothing via external feedback.

This is not rejecting validation — validation from others is a beautiful and necessary component of human experience, particularly when arriving from those who truly know and care about you. It is about not permitting that validation to become the sole condition for knowing something holds worth.

---

## Before You Read the Next Article

The next time you accomplish something you take pride in — prior to thinking about sharing it with anyone — pause for a brief moment and ask yourself:

*How do I feel about this — not what will others think, but how do I feel?*

Permit that answer to exist first, complete and real, prior to inviting anyone else in.

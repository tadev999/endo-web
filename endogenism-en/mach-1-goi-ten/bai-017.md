# Commitment Is Not Scary Because You Fear Losing Freedom — It Is Scary Because You Don't Yet Believe Your Choice Is Truly Yours

*Article 17 / Stream 1: Naming*

---

There is a question this generation hears more than any previous generation: *"Why won't you just commit?"*

Not merely in romance — though that is where the question surfaces most frequently. But in work, in direction, in choices regarding how to live. Why not stay long-term with one company? Why constantly want to try this and that? Why struggle to decide? Why continuously keep everything... open?

And young people rarely hold a clear answer — or hold an answer that sounds convincing even to themselves: *"I fear losing freedom."*

I used that sentence for a long time before realizing it wasn't the real answer. It was the easiest answer to speak — but not what was actually taking place within.

---

## The Popular Reason vs. The True Reason

When discussing fear of commitment, the most popular answer is freedom — fear of being trapped, fear of losing alternative options, fear of looking back and regretting not exploring further.

That reason isn't entirely false. Freedom is something this generation was taught to treasure — and in a world holding an abundance of options as Article 6 addressed, wanting to keep everything open is a completely understandable reaction.

Yet if it were merely fear of losing freedom, why do so many of us feel uncomfortable even while occupying that exact state of freedom? Why does avoiding commitment — though delivering more options — fail to make you feel freer, and instead leave you feeling suspended?

Perhaps fear is not about freedom. Perhaps it concerns something else — and that something needs to be faced directly.

---

## Commitment Requires Something Many Don't Yet Possess

Commitment — whether to a person, a job, or a direction — demands something very specific: you must believe that what you are choosing is truly yours.

Not choosing out of pressure. Not choosing because it is the most reasonable option according to someone else's blueprint. Not choosing because it is safest, or most expected, or least judged. But choosing because it is what you genuinely want — and believing in that enough to stand behind it.

And here lies the problem: if you don't yet know what is truly yours — if you haven't spent sufficient time asking what you genuinely want, genuinely care about, genuinely wish to build — then committing to anything becomes a gamble you lack sufficient information to place.

And gambling without sufficient information is terrifying — not because you fear losing freedom, but because you fear committing to the wrong thing. Fearing looking back and realizing you attached yourself to something not yours — wasting what cannot be recovered.

---

## Article 16 and Article 17 Meet Right Here

The previous article discussed the blueprint — what others draw for your life before you manage to ask what you want. And this article is the natural consequence of that.

When you grow up with someone else's blueprint — when major life choices are shaped by external expectations rather than internal truth — you arrive at adulthood with a very specific void: you possess little experience choosing from the inside out.

Unaccustomed to the feeling *"this is what I genuinely want"* — because that question was rarely asked. Unaccustomed to trusting your own judgment — because historically others' judgment was trusted far more. And when unaccustomed to trusting what resides within you, commitment — the act of attaching to a choice grounded in that internal truth — becomes extraordinarily difficult to execute.

Not because you fear losing freedom. But because you don't yet believe what resides inside you is trustworthy enough to attach to.

---

## When Keeping Everything Open Is Self-Protection

There is an underlying logic to keeping everything perpetually open — and when you see that logic, it no longer resembles indecisiveness. It resembles an entirely understandable strategy of self-protection.

If you don't commit to anything — you cannot commit wrong. If you don't attach deeply to anything — you cannot be hurt deeply when it doesn't turn out as hoped. If you always leave a door open — you are never fully inside anywhere, yet never fully lose that place either.

That is how the brain handles uncertainty when lacking sufficient self-knowledge to trust its choices: avoiding commitment as a means of avoiding unfixable mistakes.

The problem is that strategy carries a price — and not a small one. When committing to nothing, you genuinely possess nothing. No relationship deep enough to truly know the other. No work held long enough to master and discover meaning within. No direction clear enough to know where you are heading. You possess many options — but nothing real.

---

## What Is Required Before Committing

The remedy for fear of commitment is not *"just jump in and see what happens."* Sometimes that holds true — sometimes you only know once tried. But sometimes what is required is not committing immediately, but clarifying something first.

Specifically: beginning to build a relationship with what resides inside you.

Asking yourself the questions Article 16 posed — *is this mine, or am I following someone else's blueprint?* Noticing moments when you feel truly alive — as Articles 2 and 15 addressed. Recognizing the distinction between choices born of fear and choices born of genuine desire — as Articles 1 and 5 distinguished.

Not to arrive at a perfect answer before committing to anything. But to begin becoming acquainted with your inner voice — so that when choice arrives, you hold something to believe in.

Commitment is not terrifying when you know what you are choosing and why. It is only terrifying when you don't yet know — and the remedy for that is not avoiding commitment, but beginning to know yourself better.

---

## Not All Commitments Are Permanent

One final thing needs stating — because without it, the conversation regarding commitment easily becomes heavier than necessary.

Not every commitment is an unalterable final decision. Committing to a job doesn't mean staying forever — it means staying long enough and deeply enough to truly know if it is yours. Committing to a direction doesn't mean never altering course — it means walking far enough along that path to know where you are heading.

Commitment is not the surrender of freedom. It is the sole method to possess something real — rather than floating perpetually among possibilities without a single one becoming reality.

And when you commit to something genuinely yours — not because expected, not out of fear of error, but because you asked and listened and believed — it doesn't feel like losing freedom. It feels like being able to breathe.

---

## Before You Read the Next Article

Think about something you are currently avoiding committing to — doesn't need to be massive. Could be a project, a relationship, a direction, a small choice you continuously postpone.

And ask directly: *Am I avoiding this out of fear of losing freedom — or because I don't yet believe the choice I would make is genuinely mine?*

If it is the second question — the subsequent question isn't how to commit. But rather: *What more do I need to comprehend about myself to begin trusting my choice?*

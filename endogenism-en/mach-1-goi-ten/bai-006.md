# FOMO is not the fear of missing events — it is the fear of missing a better version of yourself.

*Article 6 / Stream 1: Naming*

---

You are home on a Friday evening.

Not because you had nowhere to go. But because you chose to stay — perhaps because you were tired, perhaps because you didn't truly want to go out, perhaps because you needed a quiet evening for yourself. At the moment you made it, that decision felt completely reasonable.

Then you open your phone. And you see someone's story, another person's photo. Someone is at a newly opened lounge. Someone else is with a group of friends you also know. Everyone looks like they are having a great evening — better than the evening you are currently having.

And the decision that once felt completely reasonable begins to waver. Not necessarily because you want to be there. But because something stirs restlessly — a feeling that you are missing out on something important.

I used to call that "fear of missing events" — until I realized that on the evenings I actually attended, that feeling didn't vanish. It simply assumed a different shape.

---

## What Is FOMO Truly Afraid Of?

Fear of Missing Out — FOMO — is often described as the anxiety of not being in the right place, at the right time, with the right people. But if that were all, why doesn't the feeling disappear when you *are* present?

You arrive at the party — and FOMO shifts into worry that there is a better party somewhere else you don't know about. You choose this job — and FOMO whispers that perhaps that other job would offer something you currently lack. You remain in this city — and FOMO suggests that if you were elsewhere, your life would look vastly different.

FOMO is not about a specific event. It is about something deeper: *the fear that somewhere out there exists a better version of your life — and you are not living it.*

Not the fear of missing a Friday night party. But the fear of missing a version of yourself — one living more authentically, more fully, more meaningfully. One who is in the right place, doing the right thing, with the right people.

Viewed through that lens, FOMO is no longer merely a bad habit born of the social media age. It is the manifestation of a larger, more painful question: *Am I living my true life?*

---

## Why Social Media Is Not the Root Cause — But the Catalyst

It is easy to blame social media. And it isn't wrong — social media has amplified FOMO to a degree no previous generation experienced.

Before social media, your scope of comparison was limited to what you actually saw — neighbors, close friends, coworkers. You didn't know what an acquaintance from 10 years ago was doing tonight. You didn't see the highlight reels of 300 people on your friends list all at once.

Now you do. And the human brain — designed to compare within a finite scope — suddenly must process a vast, continuous stream of comparative data with no endpoint.

Yet the sensation that *I am missing out on something vital* — that feeling was not created by social media. It was already there. Social media simply supplied it with endless fuel and set it running 24/7 instead of surfacing only at specific moments.

This means: turning off social media might quiet down FOMO — but it won't resolve the question lying underneath. That question will remain, waiting for you to grow quiet enough to hear it.

---

## The Trap of Infinite Choice

There is a paradox described by psychologist Barry Schwartz as the *paradox of choice*: when faced with too many options, instead of feeling freer, people often feel more anxious and less satisfied with the choice they ultimately make.

The reason is uncomplicated: when there are only one or two options, you choose and move forward. When there are ten options, you choose one — but the remaining nine exist in your mind like roads left untaken. And those untaken roads easily become the screen onto which you project everything good you imagine you lack.

Social media transformed life into an infinite menu of choices — not just regarding events, but lifestyle, values, career paths, relationship models. And with an infinite menu, it is nearly impossible to make a choice without feeling that you could have chosen better.

FOMO, in this context, is neither weakness nor lack of resolve. It is the natural reaction of a brain designed for a finite world — trying to operate within an infinite one.

---

## Where Is That "Better" Life Version?

Returning to the question at the center of FOMO: *somewhere out there exists a better version of your life.*

Here is what must be faced directly: that version does not exist.

Not because your life is currently flawless and unable to improve. But because the *better version of life* that FOMO points toward is not a physical place you can reach — it is an illusion constructed from what you see on screens, filtered through what you think you lack, and projected onto a future you never actually touch.

Because when you arrive there — when you take that other path, attend that event, show up in the right place at the right time — you are still you. With the same questions, the same voids, the same things you carry within. And FOMO will simply locate the next "better version" to point toward.

That doesn't mean all choices are equal, or that you should never change anything. But it means: what you are seeking does not reside in the event you haven't attended or the path you haven't taken. It resides in your relationship with the life you are living right now — in this choice, in this place.

---

## From FOMO to JOMO

There is a concept opposite to FOMO that I find compelling: JOMO — *Joy of Missing Out*.

This does not mean withdrawing, rejecting everything, or taking pride in non-participation. It means the capacity to remain anchored in the choice you made — without constantly looking over at other paths to see what you might be missing.

JOMO is not a state you achieve after resolving every question about life. It is what emerges when you begin believing that life is happening *here* — not in a better version waiting for you somewhere else.

And that starts not by deleting social media or compiling gratitude lists. It starts by asking: *In this moment — the moment I am actually inhabiting — is there anything real?*

Not comparing with another moment. Not evaluating whether it is "good enough." Simply asking: is there something real here?

Sometimes the answer is yes. Sometimes the answer is no — and that too is useful information, pointing toward something far more specific than merely "I need to be somewhere else."

---

## Before You Read the Next Article

The next time FOMO arises — no need to force it away.

Simply ask: *What am I truly afraid of missing? The event — or some version of myself I imagine would be there?*

That question won't resolve FOMO instantly. But it shifts the direction from outward to inward — from "where do I need to be" to "what am I searching for." And that is a far better direction to walk.

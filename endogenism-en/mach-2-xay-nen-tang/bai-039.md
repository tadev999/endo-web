# Quietness in the Heart of the City — No Need to Go Anywhere, It Is Right Here If You Know How to Find It

*Article 39 / Stream 2: Building Foundations*

---

On Friday afternoons, as the clock hand creeps toward five-thirty, the city seems to turn sound output to maximum capacity.

Engine roars at gridlocked intersections, impatient honking from people rushing home, phone rings with unprocessed messages, and intertwined thought streams in your head about unfinished checklists. Midst that thick atmosphere of commotion, a familiar longing arises: *If only I could leave everything behind. If only I could catch a ride to a remote mountain, a deserted beach, or a wooden cabin in the woods to find quietness again.*

We typically think of quietness as a location — a geographical coordinate where human sound is completely eliminated. We view it as a luxury gift appearing only during short vacations, or an ideal state reserved for those abandoning urban rhythm.

Yet life cannot pause every time your mind needs peace. Escaping the city brings only temporary relief, because when the trip ends and you return to reality, noise immediately returns — carrying the disappointment of an unfinished dream.

Endogenism philosophy posits a completely different perspective: **Quietness is not the absence of external sound, but the presence of an internal anchoring space.**

You need not book a flight ticket, wait for the next vacation, or force this city to be silent. Quietness is right here — amidst honking horns, in noisy offices, right in the busy rush — if you know how to return and open that internal space door within yourself.

---

## The Illusion of Another Place

In modern psychology, a phenomenon exists called destination addiction. This is the persistent belief that happiness, peace, or quietness always resides at the next turn: when changing to a new job, moving to another city, traveling, or retiring.

When facing urban exhaustion and noise, the mind's initial natural reaction is seeking escape. We imagine poetic scenes of a misty morning on high hills, whispering waves beside the shore, or the silent space of an ancient temple. We attribute a magical healing energy to those places, inadvertently turning our present environment into a suffocating room of commotion.

Yet Stoic philosopher Seneca wrote to his friend: *Why do you wonder that globe-trotting does not help you? Wherever you go, you take yourself along.*

If a mind packed with anxieties, expectations, and struggling internal dialogues enters a cabin in the woods, after a few hours enjoying fresh air, external quietness will inadvertently amplify internal noise. Unresolved fears, self-worth doubts, future pressures will immediately flood that silent gap.

*Why is it so quiet here yet my head keeps running?* That question likely surfaced in your mind during an expensive vacation.

Quietness found by geographical escape is merely a mirage. It relies completely on external conditions — and relying on the outside, it is fragile and easily shattered. A sudden noise, a work message sent mid-vacation, or thoughts of returning to work causes that quietness to vanish like foam.

All peace searches dependent on external circumstances represent chasing conditional value. Thinking you can be quiet only when surroundings are silent, you inadvertently hand control of your internal state to the outside world. You turn yourself into a dry leaf swept by every urban sound wind.

---

## External Noise and Internal Commotion

To find quietness amidst the city, the first step is clearly distinguishing two concepts often equated: external noise and internal commotion.

External noise encompasses all sounds and movements transpiring around you daily. Hurried traffic, colleague chatter, cafe music, rain falling on tin roofs. This is simply the natural reality of a living, moving metropolis.

Internal commotion, conversely, is the reactions, resistance, and stories your mind weaves around that noise. Irritation when someone honks loudly, insecurity hearing colleagues discuss metrics, or panic when thought streams get repeatedly interrupted.

Stoic Emperor Marcus Aurelius recorded in *Meditations* that at any moment, a human can retreat into oneself, for nowhere is quieter and freer for a human than one's own soul.

Marcus Aurelius lived in no remote monastery. He led a vast empire, constantly facing war, political intrigues, plagues, and ancient Rome's bustle. Yet he trained to construct an internal sanctuary — an internal space no external chaos could invade unless he permitted it.

From a mindfulness perspective, your mind resembles a vast sky, while noise and commotion resemble clouds or storms passing through.

The sky never attempts to chase away clouds, nor argues with storms. The sky simply contains all, embraces all, while itself retaining its pure, vast nature behind those cloud layers.

Comprehending you are the sky rather than sound clouds, you cease exhausting yourself trying to make the city silent. You realize: street honks cannot harm your core quietness, unless you permit your mind to drift into annoyance toward the honks.

## Philosophical Headphones

Imagine wearing active noise-canceling headphones and walking through a busy street.

The headphones do not stop vehicles from running, nor force people around to stop talking. What they do is emit an inverted soundwave frequency to cancel external sound vibrations, creating a soothing space for your ears.

Intrinsic quietness is precisely philosophical headphones installed inside your awareness.

Instead of canceling sound through technology, philosophical headphones cancel resistance through acceptance and presence. They permit city sounds to pass without leaving a scratch in your mind.

Look at the daily routine rhythm of an urban day.

You stand in a crowded subway car or gridlocked intersection. The person beside pushes, engines roar. The ordinary mind immediately falls into irritation: *Why is traffic so backed up? Why don't people queue? What a waste of time!*

Precisely those internal lines tear your peace apart, not the traffic jam itself.

Now, try turning on philosophical headphones. Take a deep breath and identify that traffic is backed up, vehicles move slowly, and this is reality right now. Feel your soles gripping the vehicle floor, feel breath entering and leaving your chest. Right in that moment, midst thousands rushing and stressed around, you find your own island of quietness.

Quietness lies not where sound is absent. It lies in the pause between two reactions.

That is the three-second hesitation before honking back at the person ahead. The moment you close your eyes for sixty seconds before starting a tense meeting. The moment you sip morning tea fully attentive to the cup's warmth in hand, despite app notifications rushing on your computer screen.

These quiet gaps require no hours spent meditating on a cushion. They are woven from tiny threads of attentiveness right in daily life. The city keeps moving, work keeps demanding, but at the center of that storm, you maintain self-anchoring.

## Anchoring Amidst Storms

How do we build and maintain this internal quiet space amidst a city that never sleeps? The answer lies not in complex techniques, but in daily attitude of returning to awareness.

First, practice shifting from resistance to witnessing. Whenever feeling driven crazy by surrounding noise, whether construction sounds or loud chatter, notice the internal resistance reaction: *I don't want to hear this sound!*

Release that resistance. Instead of stiffening against it, shift to the stance of a listening observer. Listen to that noise as part of urban life's natural soundscape, like ocean waves or wind rustling through forest leaves. When you cease labeling noise as an enemy, it loses power to disturb your soul.

Next, learn to anchor into the body. When the mind gets scattered and swept by city rush, it typically leaves the body and leaps into future anxiety scenarios. Return awareness to the body immediately.

Feel gravity pulling your body down onto the chair. Feel the contact of both feet on the ground. Feel your chest rising and falling with each breath. The body is always in the present. When reconnecting with the body, you immediately touch this moment's quietness.

Additionally, weave small mindfulness buffer gaps into your day. Stepping off a motorcycle or car, don't rush into the office immediately, stand still a few seconds, take a deep breath, and feel air on your facial skin. When a phone call ends, don't rush to open the next screen tab, pause ten seconds to relax stiffened shoulders. Walking from the parking lot into the building, turn those steps into a unhurried breath rhythm — merely recording the foot-touching-ground sensation with each step.

Finally, practice self-compassion toward your own commotion. Certain days, you will find yourself unable to be quiet. External noise coils around internal noise, and you fall into irritation and confusion.

Do not blame yourself for not being philosophically proper or mindful enough. Self-chastisement merely adds a fresh layer of noise. Embrace that commotion with kindness: *I am tired. The city today is too noisy, and feeling suffocated is completely natural.* Self-compassion is the softest cushion helping you regain balance.

## Quietness Was Always Here

Quietness in the heart of the city is no miracle reserved for hermits. It is an inherent psychological capacity inside every human — an intrinsic value waiting for you to recognize and awaken.

You need not run from the city seeking peace. The city with all its noise, rush, and complexity serves as your finest training ground. Where noise is greatest, the opportunity to train internal anchoring becomes deepest.

Right now, whether reading these lines on a crowded bus, in a busy cafe, or at a desk piled with papers — pause one second. Relax your forehead, relax shoulders, take a deep breath and exhale slowly.

Quietness is nowhere far. It lies not in the next trip. It is right here — beneath the noise, right inside your chest, waiting for your return.

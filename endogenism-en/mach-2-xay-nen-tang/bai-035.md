# Memento Mori — Remembering Death Is Not for Fear, but to Live with Less Regret

*Article 35 / Stream 2: Building Foundations*

---

Have you ever fallen into a day where everything around seemed to become overwhelming?

A late work notification, a nasty rumor from a colleague, a petty argument on social media, or a vague fear that you are moving slower than your peers.

Those minor details spiral deep into your mind, occupying your thoughts and leaving you feeling suffocated as if the world were collapsing.

Then suddenly, a phone call rings. You hear news that an acquaintance your age unexpectedly passed away in an accident, or a relative fell gravely ill.

In that moment, a strange wave of quietude immediately surges through your mind.

All persistent conflicts, all social media interactions, all metric pressures or reputation comparisons suddenly shrink, fade, and become completely meaningless.

You realize the things that kept you awake at night were essentially minor dust specs. And a haunting question surfaces: *If the line between life and death is so thin, what am I truly spending my life's time on?*

That sudden awakening is the essence of an ancient philosophy existing for thousands of years: Memento Mori — a Latin phrase meaning *Remember that you must die.*

In modern culture, death is a topic deemed taboo, unlucky, and frequently hidden behind sterile hospital doors.

We live as if possessing an infinite amount of time, continuously postponing what matters most, accumulating petty fears, and clinging to ephemeral extrinsic values.

This article is an invitation for you to approach Memento Mori not with gloom or fear, but as the ultimate magnifying glass helping you filter noise, awaken intrinsic value, and live a life most authentic, relaxed, and free of regret.

---

## The Illusion of Infinite Time

Why do we humans so easily fall into exhaustion over minor things, yet continuously postpone what truly holds meaning for our souls?

Existential psychologists point out that most of us live within an illusion of artificial infinity.

Because the human brain cannot visually comprehend its own ending, in the subconscious, we operate with the assumption that tomorrow we will still be here, next year we will still hold time, and after retirement we will finally begin living for ourselves.

This illusion of infinite time creates exceptionally dangerous psychological traps.

First is clinging to ephemeral things. Believing time is endless, we are willing to waste thousands of hours worrying about what others think of us, growing angry over a stranger's unpleasant look, or striving to chase extrinsic success standards we do not genuinely desire.

Next is postponing authentic living. We postpone speaking words of love to parents, postpone forgiving a relationship, postpone pursuing authentic dreams, postpone sitting quietly with ourselves.

*Whenever I'm freer... Whenever I have enough money... Whenever it's safer...*

The philosopher Seneca reminded us that we do not have a short time to live, but we waste a lot of it. Life is long enough to accomplish the greatest things if the entire time is invested wisely.

Avoiding death does not make us live longer. It merely makes us avoid authentic living as well.

---

## Two Deep Philosophical Perspectives

Memento Mori is no negative or pessimistic invention. Historically, from Roman emperors to leading Existential philosophers, remembering the finite nature of human life has always been the single most powerful tool for building internal strength.

In *Meditations*, Roman Emperor Marcus Aurelius continuously reminded himself each morning: *You could leave life right now. Let that determine what you do and say and think.*

For Stoic philosophers, Memento Mori was a moral and intellectual ruler. When standing before a difficult decision, or when tempted by greed, anger, envy: place that decision on the scales of Memento Mori.

If you knew today might be the final day you breathe, would you still want to spend hours arguing who is right or wrong online? Would you still want to hold bitter anger toward a loved one? Would you still weary yourself wearing the suit of fake perfection to please the public?

Death does not render life meaningless. On the contrary, precisely the finite nature of death bestows upon each present second invaluable worth.

From an existential philosophy perspective, thinker Martin Heidegger spoke of authentic living when human beings courageously face the reality that death is an absolutely personal experience. No one can die for you.

Most people drift with the crowd, doing what society dictates, thinking with others' thoughts.

Only when awakening before life's finite nature do you break free from the crowd's trance. Awareness of death liberates you from reliance on public opinion.

It returns to you the supreme right of self-determination: This is my own life, and I must take full responsibility for how I live it.

## Metaphors of the Hourglass and the Exhibition Ticket

To keep Memento Mori from becoming a dry theory, try imagining two metaphors below in your life.

Imagine your life as a clear glass hourglass. Each grain of sand flowing down represents a second, a minute, a day passing that you never recover.

What is notable is this hourglass is placed in a dim room, and the top glass containing remaining sand is covered by a black cloth. You hold no idea whether thousands of grains remain inside, or merely a final few.

If you knew with certainty that top sand could flow for only a few hours more, what would you do?

You would never sit there agonizing over sand grains that fell ten years ago. Nor would you panic worrying about sand grains ten years later.

You would treasure each individual sand grain gently falling right in this moment. You would look deeply into the eyes of the person before you, savor the hot tea cup in hand, listen to rain falling outside the porch with the fullest presence.

Memento Mori is the act of lifting that black cloth to look at that hourglass each day.

Now, imagine purchasing a ticket to visit a beautiful art museum. The ticket holds a clear rule: The museum closes at sunset, and all visitors must depart when the chime rings. You do not know precisely when sunset will arrive.

What would a wise visitor do?

They would not spend all time standing in the hallway angry because floor tiles hold a few scratches, or arguing with the guard about service attitude.

They would walk swiftly into galleries, admiring each painting with full wonder, enjoying beauty, and savoring each moment before closing time arrives.

This life is precisely that art exhibition. Experiences, relationships, natural beauty, and human emotions are those precious paintings.

Death is simply the chime signaling closing time. Remembering that chime does not make paintings less beautiful; it makes each second you stand before the painting sacred and complete a thousand times over.

## Bringing the Reminder into Daily Life

Practicing Memento Mori does not mean pasting gloomy images across your room or living in shallow panic burning energy on hedonistic parties.

Genuine Memento Mori brings relaxation, depth, and composure through daily mindful pauses.

First is the morning alignment habit.

Each morning upon waking, before reaching for your phone to get drawn into notifications and social news, spend 60 seconds sitting quietly on your bed.

Take a deep breath and say to yourself: *That I opened my eyes this morning and feel my chest breathing is a wonderful privilege. Today might be the final day I live on this earth. I will choose to live today with the utmost kindness, honesty, and completeness. I will not waste today on useless anger or fear.*

This brief reminder serves as psychological armor, helping you step into the new day with composure, not easily shaken by minor mishaps.

Next is the Memento Mori filter for decisions and relationships.

When falling into a tense argument, a persistent grudge with a loved one, or anxiety over money or reputation, activate the filter with the question: *If this were the final week I lived on earth, would this matter so much?*

If the answer is no — and most things fall into this box — smile at the problem's smallness, release meaningless right-and-wrong, and reclaim internal peace.

If the answer is yes — like healing a wound, apologizing, staying with loved ones, or living true to core values — act immediately without postponement.

Finally is ultimate compassion toward others.

When remembering that you yourself will die, you immediately realize a moving truth: everyone around you — loved ones, strangers, and those irritating you — all carry the finite sentence of human life.

The colleague snapping at you will also age and depart life. The friend you envy will also face dissolution.

When seeing finitude in others, anger and envy within you automatically melt, making room for deep compassion.

You view human beings not through their rigid Ego armor, but through the fragility of life. You forgive easier, tolerate easier, knowing time together on this planet is exceedingly brief.

## The Supreme Freedom of Intrinsic Value

Modern psychology indicates that fear of death is essentially a reflection of the fear of never having truly lived.

When a person lives a life dependent entirely on extrinsic value — perpetually chasing others' validation, wearing social masks, and forgetting their soul — they will immensely fear death, because they feel they never began living.

But when you return to intrinsic value — when you realize your worth lies in conscious presence itself, in capacity to love, in self-honesty each moment — death ceases to be a terrifying enemy.

Memento Mori returns supreme freedom to you.

When no longer fearing death, you no longer fear public judgment, no longer fear temporary failure, no longer fear rejection.

You become fearless — not because you are reckless, but because you comprehend universe laws and treasure each step you take.

Live today as if it were a sole gift, a unique masterpiece you are permitted to create.

So that when the final hour arrives, whenever it be, you can smile gently looking back and say: *I lived completely. I hold no regret.*

# Change Does Not Start from a Major Turning Point — It Starts from the Small Moment You Choose to Walk in the Right Direction, Even if Only a Little

*Article 61 / Stream 3: Small Actions*

---

We often live with a soothing yet illusory myth about change.

That is the myth of grand leaps — waking up one morning and feeling like a completely different person, a dramatic New Year's resolution tearing up the entire past, a soul-saving journey far away, or a shocking event permanently shaking our life's structure.

We wait for that turning point like someone walking in the night waiting for a lightning flash across the sky to illuminate the full road.

Yet the lightning comes only in a moment, then returns the sky to vast darkness. And you realize you still stand right in the old spot, with tired feet and an even more disappointed mind.

Stream 3 of this series — Small Actions — opens to liberate you from the burden of those grand illusions.

Because endogenous philosophy views transformation in a completely different way: True change does not start from a grand turning point. It starts from the micro moment when you choose to walk in the right direction, even if only a little.

---

## The Illusion of Grand Leaps

The human mind has a very natural tendency: it likes what is sensational, fast, and performative.

When feeling one's life falling into a deadlock, the ego's initial reaction is typically imagining a grand plan. You decide starting tomorrow to wake at five in the morning, run ten kilometers, read a book each week, cut off all bad habits, and alter full dietary regimes.

*This time I must do it big. Must completely transform to be meaningful...*

Yet what happens after two or three days of exhausting effort?

Your body protests, your mind tires out, and old habits rush back with double force. Failing to maintain that giant goal, you immediately conclude you lack discipline, are a failure, and your life can never change.

That is precisely the leap illusion trap. Setting too large a demand, you create too wide a gap between reality and desire. That gap generates fear and paralysis.

Your brain views overly abrupt change as a threat to inherent safety. It triggers self-defense mechanisms, pulling you back to what is most familiar — even if that familiarity is hurting you.

Major change demands a giant energy volume that your internal foundation has not yet accumulated sufficiently. It resembles striving to erect a high-rise building on uncompacted soil.

---

## The Compass's One-Degree Tilt

Imagine a ship setting sail on the open sea, a journey spanning thousands of nautical miles across vast ocean waters.

If the captain adjusts the helm to tilt the ship's course by merely a single degree — an exceptionally small angle, almost imperceptible to the naked eye in that moment — what will happen?

In the initial few minutes, the ship still appears to sail on the same path with other ships. In the initial few hours, the deviation distance remains negligible.

Yet after thousands of nautical miles across the ocean, that initial one-degree tilt will bring the ship to shore on a completely different continent, thousands of kilometers away from the original destination.

Your internal shift operates according to that exact one-degree tilt principle.

Change demands not turning around one hundred eighty degrees immediately. It demands merely that you courageously create a small tilt in how you think, react, or choose right in this moment.

When someone utters an insulting word to you, instead of immediately reacting with habitual anger, you choose to pause a second before speaking. That is a tilt.

When feeling exhausted after a long day, instead of hastily grabbing your phone scrolling mindlessly for two full hours, you choose to place the phone down and close eyes taking three deep breaths. That is a tilt.

These choices appear so small that your ego whispers: *What does doing this resolve anyway? Everything remains as bad as before.*

Yet the ego was mistaken. Smallness means not holding no power. That one-degree tilt is precisely where freedom is reborn.

---

## Silent Accumulation Beneath Deep Soil

Nature is never in a hurry, yet everything gets completed via small actions accumulated over time.

Look at how a water drop falls onto a hard limestone rock surface. A single water drop cannot crack the rock. Thousands of falling water drops seem also to leave no worn trace.

Yet if that water drop patiently falls on the exact spot, day after day, month after month, water's softness will ultimately pierce through rock's hardness.

Or look at how a bamboo sprout erupts beneath the ground. In the initial four years after sowing seeds, you will see no green shoot rising above the soil surface. However much you water, fertilize, or carefully tend, the ground remains completely silent.

*Seems I am doing a useless task... No change is occurring at all...*

Yet beneath that quiet deep soil layer, the bamboo seed is silently plunging thousands of small roots spreading wide around, forming a network clinging sturdily to the ground's heart.

And then coming to the fifth year, the bamboo erupts rising at astonishing speed — it can grow nearly a meter each day and reach dozens of meters in height in just a few short weeks.

Your internal transformation also needs such a "lying beneath deep soil" period.

Small actions you choose to do daily — tidying a desk corner, drinking a warm water glass in silence, writing down a reflective line, or declining an inappropriate invitation — seem not to create any miraculous change immediately.

Yet they are silently compacting your internal soil. They are nourishing awareness root systems beneath consciousness surfaces.

Judge not practice effectiveness via external sensational outputs. Judge it via internal rooting depth.

---

## Small Actions' True Nature

Why does endogenous philosophy center on "Small Actions" rather than macro strategies?

Because small actions possess an exceptionally miraculous trait: bypassing fear's defense wall.

Telling yourself you must write a three-hundred-page book, your mind panics and seeks every way to procrastinate. Yet telling yourself you need write merely a single sentence, your mind smiles and nods in agreement.

Telling yourself you must clean the entire messy house, you feel tired right before starting. Yet telling yourself you need merely pick up a jacket and hang it in the closet, psychological barriers collapse immediately.

A small action is no reduced portion of a big task. A small action is a living attitude.

That is the attitude respecting present reality. That is the acknowledgment that you live not in the grand future you dream of, nor in the painful past you regret. You live merely in this moment, with small tools held in hand.

Executing a small action with full attentiveness, you send a very powerful signal to your subconscious: *I am a person capable of mastering my life. I need not wait for perfect conditions to begin.*

Each small action is a vote cast for the human version you wish to become.

You become a peaceful person not through reading a hundred books on mindfulness. You become a peaceful person when choosing to take a deep breath in the moment pressure strikes.

You become a free person not through a noisy revolution. You become a free person thanks to small, quiet, yet right-direction decisions repeated daily.

---

## Recognizing the Moment You Choose the Right Direction

How do you know you are walking in the right direction, when external outputs haven't appeared yet?

The answer lies not in others' praise, nor in success metrics measured by the world.

The answer lies in the lightened and clear sensation rising inside you right after making the choice.

That is the moment standing before two paths: the old familiar path with destructive natural reflexes, and the new unfamiliar path bringing kindness to self.

The old path urges: *Blame away! Indulge away! Lie down scrolling phones to forget life!*

Yet you pause. You see that urge. And you choose to turn to the new path — even if taking merely a half-foot step.

*I know where the old path leads me. Today, let me nudge a bit toward the light to see...*

That moment can happen in a dark kitchen corner, when washing a dish after meals instead of letting it accumulate in the sink.

That moment can happen when preparing to explode in anger at children or relatives, yet choosing to step onto the balcony gazing at the sky thirty seconds.

That moment can happen when feeling completely exhausted, yet instead of chastising oneself over unproductive work, choosing to pull the blanket and telling yourself resting today is a kind act.

Disregard not those moments. Those are no trivial behaviors. Those are true turning points — turning points holding no noise, no audience, yet capable of re-shaping your soul's full trajectory.

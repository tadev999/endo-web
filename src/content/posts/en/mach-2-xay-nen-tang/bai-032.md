# There Are Things You Cannot Control — And Here Is What You Can Always Control

*Article 32 / Stream 2: Building Foundations*

---

There is a day when nothing goes according to plan.

Not a catastrophe. Not a major crisis. Simply a series of minor mishaps adding up — a meeting postponed last minute, someone failing to respond as hoped, a carefully prepared plan suddenly needing alteration. Nothing is entirely wrong, yet nothing is right in the manner you desired. And by day's end, a very specific exhaustion sets in — tired not from doing much, but tired from striving to control things that refuse to be controlled.

I spent many such days before realizing I was expending energy on precisely the things that, no matter how much energy expended, would not alter — while ignoring the single thing always within reach.

---

## The Circles of Control

There is an old framework yet still immensely useful — simple enough to remember, deep enough to transform how you spend mental energy.

Imagine two concentric circles.

The outer circle represents everything *outside your control*: the outcomes of efforts you put forth, others' reactions, external events, the weather — in both literal and figurative senses. You can influence certain items within this circle, but never control them completely.

The inner circle represents everything *always within your control*: how you respond to what occurs, the values you choose to act upon, where you direct your attention, how you treat yourself and others in any given circumstance.

Most human anxiety, stress, and frustration energy gets spent on the outer circle — on things they cannot control. And meanwhile, the inner circle — what is genuinely always within reach — goes frequently ignored.

---

## Why We Keep Striving to Control the Uncontrollable

Knowing about the circles of control is not difficult. Applying it in practice is far harder. And the reason is not that we lack discipline — but because very real psychological reasons exist why the human brain leans toward things outside control.

**The human brain is wired to monitor threats** — and threats typically arrive from the outside. When someone fails to reply to your message, the brain does not read that as "an external event outside control." It reads it as "a potential issue needing immediate resolution." And it expends energy there — not because you are irrational, but because the brain's early warning system is doing its job.

**Control generates a feeling of safety** — even when that control is an illusion. Striving to control others' reactions, striving to control situational outcomes, striving to plan for every possible scenario — all generate a vague sensation of doing something, of not being completely helpless. Even though they do not actually alter anything.

**Lacking control feels like failure** — particularly in a culture prizing proactivity and the capacity to generate results. If things do not proceed as you desired, the brain automatically asks: *what did I do wrong? Where did I fail to control well enough?* And from there, letting go feels like surrendering, like passivity, like not trying hard enough.

---

## Letting Go Is Not Giving Up

This is the most vital point in this article — and also the point most frequently misunderstood.

Focusing on the inner circle does not mean not caring about what happens outside. It does not mean passivity, accepting everything blindly, or making no effort toward better outcomes.

It means: you do everything within your reach — then you let go of the part outside your reach.

Not letting go prior to making an effort. Not letting go out of apathy. But letting go *after* doing what you could do — because continuing to grip tightly what cannot be controlled does not alter the outcome, it merely exhausts you.

The image I find useful: a farmer sowing seeds. They do everything within reach — selecting good soil, sowing at the right moment, watering regularly, weeding. But they cannot control the weather, cannot force seeds to sprout according to wish, cannot guarantee a good harvest regardless of circumstances. If after doing everything possible, they still sit worrying about what cannot be controlled — that anxiety does not make the crop better. It merely exhausts the farmer.

Letting go, in this sense, is not giving up. It is a method of preserving energy — to continue doing well what is within reach, rather than expending it on what lies outside reach.

---

## What Is Always Within Your Control

So what does the inner circle — what is always within control — look like in practice?

**How you respond, not what occurs.** The other person spoke those words — you did not choose those words. But you choose the pause between hearing those words and responding. That pause is small — yet within it resides the entire difference between an automated reaction and a conscious response.

**The values you act upon.** No matter how circumstances alter, you can still choose to act according to what matters to you — honesty, care, patience. This is something no one can take away no matter how bad external circumstances become.

**Your attention.** You cannot control which thoughts surface in your mind — but you can choose which thoughts to pursue further, which thoughts to let pass. This is a skill requiring practice, not something instant — but it is something always within reach.

**How you care for yourself on difficult days.** When everything outside strays from your wish, you can still choose whether to treat yourself with harshness or compassion. This is a small choice yet impacts everything else.

---

## When Letting Go Becomes Truly Possible

There is something many realize after practicing distinguishing these two circles: letting go of the outer circle is not merely a matter of discipline. It also depends on where you stand within.

When acting from fear — fear of failure, fear of inadequacy, fear of being left behind — letting go of the outer circle is extraordinarily hard, because the outer circle is where all evidence of your adequacy or inadequacy resides. Project outcomes, others' reactions, numbers on a screen — all those things become excessively important because they bear a task not theirs: defining your worth.

But when your worth does not depend on external outcomes — when you begin trusting that who you are does not alter regardless of outcomes — the outer circle becomes far less terrifying. Not because you don't care about outcomes. But because outcomes, whatever they be, are no longer what defines who you are.

That is when letting go no longer needs to be forced — it becomes the natural occurrence when you no longer require the outer circle to feel okay.

## Before You Read the Next Article

Today, try noticing — without judgment — where you expend mental energy most.

Is most of it directed toward items in the outer circle — things you strive to influence but cannot truly control?

And if you shifted a portion of that energy into the inner circle — into how you respond, into the values you choose to act upon, into how you care for yourself today — what might be different?

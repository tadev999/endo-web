# "Healing" — Or Another Way to Avoid Facing What Is Really Happening Inside You?

*Article 20 / Stream 1: Naming*

---

There is a word that appeared multiple times in previous articles — and it is time to face it directly.

*Healing.*

This word has surfaced everywhere over recent years — in social media captions, course titles, the way people describe what they are doing with their lives. *"I am on a healing journey." "This is my year of healing." "I need time to heal."*

There is nothing wrong with that word. Healing is real — and necessary. Yet there is a question worth posing, not to dismiss the concept of healing, but to prevent it from becoming the exact opposite of its original meaning:

*When I say I am healing — am I genuinely facing what needs to be healed, or am I using that word as a way to avoid facing it?*

I spent time using the word "healing" for many things I never actually looked at directly.

---

## When a Word Becomes a Shield

Language possesses a curious characteristic: a word initially holding very specific and profound meaning can, through time and overuse, lose its weight and become a convenient shield.

"Healing" initially described a very specific process: staring directly at a wound, feeling its pain, understanding where it came from, and gradually — not quickly, not easily — learning to carry it in a different way, or letting it settle with time and proper care.

Yet as the word grew popular, it began being applied to many different things — some genuinely healing, others not quite:

Sometimes *"I need to heal"* actually means *"I need to rest."* You just passed through a stressful period; body and mind need time to recover — no specific wound needs dissecting, you simply need to slow down. That is completely valid, just not healing in the deep sense the word initially carried.

Sometimes it means *"I want to buy something to feel better."* A trip, an item, a spa day, a new course. Not wrong either — those can bring genuine joy. But that is consumption, not healing — and calling it healing can trick you into thinking you are addressing something internal when you are merely enjoying temporary comfort.

And sometimes — this is the hardest part to state — it means *"I am avoiding facing what actually hurts by naming a vague process and never truly beginning it."* You speak of healing after a broken relationship — yet never sit with the specific pain of losing that person, continuously finding new things to fill the void. You speak of healing childhood trauma — yet never name specifically what occurred, using generic phrases like "toxic energy" or "inner wounds" without ever going deeper.

---

## The Difference Between Genuine Healing and Shielded Avoidance

There is a way to distinguish — not flawless, but useful: genuine healing is usually uncomfortable before it feels relieving. Shielded avoidance is usually comfortable from the outset, and never truly leads anywhere else.

Genuine healing requires feeling what you have avoided feeling. It means sitting with sadness rather than immediately finding an exit. It means naming precisely what hurt you — not a vague, palatable version of it, but the specific truth, however difficult to look at. It means, at times, facing the people or situations that caused the wound — not necessarily in person, but at least in your own mind.

Shielded avoidance looks very different — though from the outside it can look identical. It continuously seeks new experiences to feel better without ever pausing long enough to ask why it needs to feel better so continuously. It speaks of wounds using abstract language — "negative energy," "low vibrations" — without ever naming specifically what occurred. It builds an identity around "being in healing" without ever actually progressing — because progress means one day no longer needing that identity.

---

## Why Avoidance Is Understandable — Not for Judgment

It must be stated clearly: if you recognize yourself in the avoidance part — that does not mean you are weak or insincere. Avoidance is a very natural protective mechanism, and it exists for a logical reason.

Facing a wound directly requires feeling it — and feeling it means temporarily feeling worse before feeling better. That is a trade-off not easily accepted, especially when already exhausted and simply wanting to feel okay.

And sometimes, not being ready to face something is not wrong — it is real. Certain wounds require time before you are stable enough to look at them. Forcing yourself to face things too early, before possessing sufficient internal and external resources to process them, can cause more harm than good.

The issue isn't that avoidance exists. The issue is when avoidance is called healing — because then, you think you are progressing when you are actually standing still, or worse, walking in circles around the identical spot forever.

---

## Questions to Distinguish

Here are a few questions that can help distinguish — not to judge yourself, but to cultivate necessary honesty:

*Can I name specifically what I am healing — or does it remain vague after a long time?* Genuine healing, over time, renders the wound clearer, not vaguer.

*Am I feeling anything uncomfortable during this process — or is everything pleasant and comfortable?* Healing isn't constantly painful — but if a process continues long without a single difficult moment, it may not have touched the wound.

*Am I progressing — however slowly — or am I in the exact spot I occupied a year ago?* Genuine healing, though non-linear, shows movement over time. Avoidance keeps you in place, even if the surface looks active.

*Do I need to continuously talk about healing to feel like I am healing?* This is the hardest question — because sometimes talking about the process is a necessary component. But when talking replaces doing, it is a signal worth noting.

---

## Facing It Does Not Mean Doing It Alone

There is a common misconception about "facing it directly" — that it means handling everything by yourself, needing no help, as stoic as possible.

That is false. Facing things directly frequently requires support — from professionals, from trusted friends, from a community understanding what you undergo. The difference isn't whether help is present. It lies in whether you use that help to face things — or use it as another way to avoid looking yourself.

A skilled therapist doesn't supply comfortable answers to make you feel good immediately — they help you look at things you avoided looking at. A true friend doesn't merely speak comforting words — they possess the courage to pose hard questions. Genuine support doesn't make facing things easier emotionally — it makes facing things possible, because you don't have to do it entirely alone.

Facing things directly, when taking place, rarely feels like a "breakthrough" as depicted in movies or self-help books. It is typically much quieter — and uncomfortable. It might be a conversation where you speak what was never put into words, feeling shaky afterward rather than instantly relieved. It might be a moment you permit yourself to cry over something you previously dismissed with "it's fine" — feeling more exhausted than liberated. Relief usually arrives later, not during the moment of facing it. Knowing that prevents giving up midway when facing things directly fails to deliver the immediate comfort expected.

---

## Before You Read the Next Article

Think about something you are calling "healing" — a past relationship, a difficult period, a childhood wound.

And ask with complete honesty: *Can I name specifically what I am healing? Am I feeling it — or am I avoiding it with a positive-sounding label?*

No need for a perfect answer. And if the answer is *"I am avoiding it"* — that is no failure. That is the honesty needed to begin the true process, when you are ready.

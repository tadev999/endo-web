# There Is a Pressure Called "Having to Act Okay" — And It Is Heavier Than Any Other Pressure

*Article 22 / Stream 1: Naming*

---

There is a question asked dozens of times each week, where almost no one genuinely wants to hear the real answer.

*"How have you been lately?"*

And there is a response almost automatic, requiring no thought, springing forth before you manage to check whether it holds true:

*"Good. Normal."*

Occasionally that holds true. But numerous times, it does not — and you know it doesn't the moment it leaves your lips. Inside, something is far from okay. Yet the response "good" has become a reflex, faster than thought, faster than truth.

I have spoken "good" in a completely casual voice while inside was a tangled mess — and the terrifying part wasn't lying, but lying so effortlessly that it no longer felt like lying.

---

## When "Good" Becomes the Default Vocabulary

"Good" is not always deliberate concealment. Most of the time, it is a social convention — a rapid answer to a question where the asker genuinely expects no lengthy reply. When a colleague asks "how are you" while walking down the hallway, they aren't inviting you into a deep conversation — they are performing a social ritual. And "good" is the proper script for that ritual.

The problem isn't the convention itself. The problem is when that convention expands beyond casual social situations — and becomes the default answer even when someone is genuinely asking, genuinely holding time to listen, genuinely caring.

When that occurs, "good" ceases to be a social convention. It becomes a wall — and that wall, built high enough and long enough, begins separating you from the very people who might truly be there for you.

---

## Where the Pressure Originates

No one sits down to instruct you to act okay at all times. Yet that lesson is transmitted through numerous subtle channels — small enough for no one to notice they are teaching it, yet repeated often enough to seep deep.

There is pressure stemming from *not wanting to be a burden to others*. Everyone is busy, holding their own troubles — and bringing one's own issues to others feels like piling additional weight onto their shoulders. Thus, the most "polite" path appears to be keeping everything inside.

There is pressure stemming from *fear of being perceived as weak*. In many environments — corporate, familial, peer groups — "strength" is highly prized, and strength is frequently misunderstood as never revealing difficulty. Admitting one is not okay feels like admitting defeat.

There is pressure stemming from *disbelieving that your truth will be received well*. Perhaps previously, when you attempted honesty, the reaction received was not listening but discomfort, a rushed attempt to fix things, or worse, judgment. And after a few such instances, one learns that speaking truth is unsafe — saying "good" is far safer.

And there is pressure from within oneself — believing that admitting one is not okay renders it more real, heavier, harder to reverse. As if maintaining silence were the method to keep the problem small.

---

## The Price of Continuously Acting Okay

There is a painful paradox regarding constantly saying "good": it is employed as a means of self-protection — yet over time, becomes the root of deeper isolation.

When you constantly say good, others begin believing you are good. Not because they don't care — but because you consistently transmitted signals indicating no need to ask further. And gradually, even those who genuinely care about you learn not to probe deeper — because the answer is always identical, so asking further feels pointless, or like violating boundaries you established.

The result is a hard-to-break loop: you don't speak truthfully for fear of bothering or appearing weak — others stop asking deeply believing you are fine — and you feel lonelier, because no one truly knows what is transpiring inside — and that loneliness reinforces the belief that truth is unsafe, since it seems no one is truly there to listen.

This is precisely the type of loneliness occurring right in the midst of connection — not from lack of surrounding people, but because none of them know the truth since the truth was never spoken.

---

## "Having to Act Okay" and the Relationship with Oneself

There is a less discussed dimension of this pressure: it doesn't merely impact how you communicate with others. It impacts how you communicate with yourself.

When you say "good" often enough to others, you begin saying "good" to yourself — even when no one else is listening. You learn the reflex of denial rather than acknowledgment, to the point where even in solitary moments, when holding no audience, no one to impress, you still automatically push away uncomfortable emotions before they manage to be acknowledged.

This is where social pressure and the relationship with internal emotions intersect — creating a subtle spiral hard to detect: you don't speak truth to others out of fear of their reaction, and after long enough, you no longer speak truth to yourself, because pushing away reality has become an automated habit applied to outsiders and self alike.

---

## Honesty Does Not Mean Exposing Everything

One thing must be clarified — because otherwise, this essay could easily be misunderstood as a call to share every emotion with everyone at all times.

It is not. A vital distinction exists between *honesty* and *exposure*.

Honesty doesn't mean recounting every detail to anyone who asks. It means, with appropriate people and at appropriate times, not automatically selecting the safest answer when that answer is false. It means holding at least a few individuals in life — no need for many — with whom you permit yourself to say "actually, things aren't great" when that is the truth.

You don't need to open up to everyone. But you require at least one space — a person, a journal, a moment with yourself — where the answer "good" isn't an automated reflex, but a genuine choice made after honestly asking oneself that question first.

---

## Before You Read the Next Article

The next time someone asks *"how have you been lately?"* — before answering, take one second to genuinely ask yourself that question.

Not to alter the response immediately — you might still choose to say "good" because it is neither the time nor the person to share deeply, and that is completely fine. But at least know your own true answer — even if not yet ready to speak it aloud to others.

*Am I truly okay? And if not — do I hold anyone with whom I can speak of it?*

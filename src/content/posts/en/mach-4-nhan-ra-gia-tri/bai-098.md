# What you call a weakness is sometimes what others see as your strength — and you have never allowed yourself to look at it that way.

*Article 98 / Stream 4: Recognizing Value*

---

We are our own harshest judges.

From a young age, we start compiling a list of our personal "system errors" — things we label as weaknesses: being overly sensitive, shy in front of a crowd, prone to overthinking, or stubbornly unwilling to compromise.

We spend so much time and energy trying to fix, hide, or eliminate these "weaknesses." We believe that only by getting rid of them can we become a perfect, more lovable version of ourselves.

But have you ever stopped and asked yourself: **What do these weaknesses actually look like through a different lens?**

Here's a surprising truth: the thing you hate most about yourself is sometimes the very thing others admire most about you. What you call a "flaw" is often the unique trait that creates your strength and intrinsic worth — you simply have never allowed yourself to see it from that angle.

---

## The Dual Nature of Every Personal Trait

In psychology, no personality trait is purely a "strength" or a "weakness." Every trait is a double-edged sword, a spectrum with two poles. The difference lies in context and in how we name it.

Let's place a few familiar "weaknesses" on the scale and look at their other side:

### 1. Over-sensitivity
- **Through the lens of self-judgment**: You see yourself as weak, easily hurt, prone to crying, and too preoccupied with other people's feelings. You hate that you can't be "thick-skinned" or colder.
- **Through the lens of others**: They see in you **deep empathy**, excellent listening skills, and rare sensitivity. You're the first to notice they're sad even when they're trying to seem fine. Your presence makes the people around you feel safe, because they know you won't judge them.

### 2. Shyness/Introversion
- **Through the lens of self-judgment**: You see yourself as bad at socializing, passive, dull at parties, and unable to shine in front of a crowd.
- **Through the lens of others**: They see in you **calmness, steadiness, and reliability**. When you speak, your words carry weight because you've thought carefully before saying them. You're a sharp observer, able to notice details that louder people tend to miss.

### 3. Overthinking
- **Through the lens of self-judgment**: You see your own mind as a curse, constantly conjuring worst-case scenarios and unable to stop analyzing everything.
- **Through the lens of others**: They see in you **thoughtfulness, carefulness, and foresight**. You always have backup plans ready and the ability to solve complex problems thanks to your deep analytical thinking.

---

## The Illusion of Smooth, Even Roundness

Why is it so hard for us to accept these traits in ourselves?

Because modern society loves things "smooth and even." The education and hiring systems are designed to produce individuals with a uniform skill set, with no edge too sharp or too uneven. We're taught to sand ourselves down to fit into those molds.

But real life doesn't run like a factory assembly line. The people who create real change, who bring deep warmth, or who have extraordinary creative ability are usually people with a psychological structure full of "bumps and hollows."

The very protrusion you call a "weakness" is actually your unique anchor point with the world. If you sand down every edge of yourself to become perfectly round, you also cut away the most distinctive part of who you are.

---

## A Practice for Shifting Your Lens

To begin accepting these traits, you need to take a step of redefining your own language:

1. Write down the **3 traits** you dislike most about yourself (the things you always want to change or hide).
2. For each trait, find a more positive word to describe it, based on the value it brings to others.
   - Example: *"Overly anxious"* → *"Thoughtful and careful."*
   - Example: *"Stubborn"* → *"Firm in your personal principles."*
3. Ask a close friend or someone who loves you: **"What do you see in me that's most unique or most worth cherishing?"** You'll be surprised to find their answer often matches exactly what you've been trying to hide.

You don't need to become a flawless version of yourself to have worth. Your core value lies in learning to reconcile with every different part of yourself, looking at them with understanding and compassion.
